Chemotherapy: how it works, what a cycle is like, and how side effects are managed

Medically reviewed by Dr. Ayush Shashikant Dubey · Written by PharmEasy Medical Team · Published 6 November 2026 · Updated on 6 November 2026
Your pathSymptoms› Tests and diagnosis› Stages and types› Treatment › Medicines› Living with it

Chemotherapy is a treatment which uses many different medicines to kill cancer cells, which are rapidly dividing.[1] Doctors select specific medicines or combinations based on the type of cancer, using evidence from years of research and clinical trials. For example, the medicines used to treat lymphoma can be very different from those used for colon cancer;[2], [3] therefore, the side effects and overall experience of chemotherapy vary from person to person and depend on the treatment prescribed.

India recorded an estimated 14,61,427 cancer cases in 2022.[4] Chemotherapy is an important treatment for many cancers. In some cases, it helps shrink a tumour before surgery or destroy cancer cells that remain after an operation. For certain cancers, it can also help cure the condition[1].

An important thing to know: The side effects you experience cannot tell you how well chemotherapy is treating your cancer. Having fewer side effects does not mean the treatment is less effective, and having more side effects does not mean it is working better. There is no relation between side effects of the treatment and its effectiveness[1].

How it works

Cancer cells usually grow and multiply faster than most normal cells. Chemotherapy uses medicines that interfere with this process to damage or destroy cancer cells. Different medicines work in different ways and target different stages of cell growth and division. Therefore, doctors may combine several medicines to treat cancer more effectively. The following are a few examples:

  • Alkylating Agents: These medicines damage DNA (the material that carries genetic instructions), making it harder for cancer cells to grow and multiply.
  • Antimetabolites: These medicines resemble substances cells need to make DNA. They interfere with DNA production, making it harder for cancer cells to grow and multiply.
  • Antimicrotubular Agents: These medicines disrupt microtubules, which are structures that help cells divide. This prevents cancer cells from dividing properly and may cause them to die.
  • Antitumour Antibiotics: These medicines interfere with DNA or RNA (the material involved in making proteins), preventing cancer cells from growing and multiplying. Examples include bleomycin and dactinomycin.
  • Miscellaneous Agents: These medicines work through other mechanisms to affect cancer cell growth or development. For example, hydroxyurea interferes with DNA production, while tretinoin helps certain abnormal blood cells mature.[5]

When a cell suffers damage that it cannot repair, it may activate a natural process that causes the cell to die. However, chemotherapy can also affect healthy cells that grow and divide quickly. These include cells in the bone marrow, the lining of the mouth and digestive tract, and hair follicles. Damage to these healthy cells explains why chemotherapy can cause side effects such as low blood cell counts, mouth sores, digestive problems and hair loss[1], [5].

How it is given

Chemotherapy is usually given by seven routes.

  • Oral Chemotherapy: This can be taken as tablets or pills at home[1].
  • Injections: These may be given through muscle or under the skin. An intramuscular injection delivers the medicine into a muscle, usually in the arm, thigh, or hip. A subcutaneous injection delivers it into the fatty tissue just beneath the skin, such as in the abdomen, arm or thigh. The injection site depends on the medicine being used[6].
  • Intravenous Infusion (IV): This is the most commonly used method. The medicine is given through a vein in the arm or through a central line. A PICC line or a small port (chemoport)[1], [7] may be placed under the skin of the chest when treatment is expected to last long, or the medicines can irritate the smaller veins.
  • Topical Chemotherapy: This is usually in the form of a cream, mostly for some skin cancers.
  • Intrathecal Chemotherapy: This is usually given into the cerebrospinal fluid, using a procedure called lumbar puncture. This is because most medicines won't be able to cross the blood-brain barrier to reach the brain.
  • Intraperitoneal Chemotherapy: This is given directly into the space inside the abdomen that surrounds organs such as the stomach, intestines and liver.
  • Intra-Arterial: This is given into the artery that carries blood to a tumour[1].

What to expect during a chemotherapy session: Blood counts are usually checked a day before treatment. A cannula or port needle is used to administer the medicines. Anti-sickness medicines and, when needed, steroids or allergy-prevention medicines are given first, usually for the first few minutes. Chemotherapy may take a few minutes to several hours, depending on the medicine. After the line is flushed, most people can go home the same day[6].

What a course looks like

Chemotherapy is usually delivered in cycles, with treatment followed by a resting period. For example, a cycle may involve treatment for two weeks, followed by a one-week break. This pattern may is repeated every 21 days[1].

The break is an important part of the treatment, not just a gap. It helps in the growth and recovery of healthy tissues such as bone marrow, gut lining, and hair follicles[1]. Cancer cells may recover less due to the damage caused by chemotherapy. Following the planned schedule helps balance cancer treatment with the recovery of healthy cells.

Doctors may delay a cycle or lower the dose if blood cell counts remain low or side effects worsen. These changes help keep treatment safe and do not mean it has failed. Doctors assess the response through physical examinations and blood tests at intervals[8].

The medicines used

Doctors prescribe chemotherapy medicines by their generic names. Knowing which group a medicine belongs to can help patients understand the side effects it may cause.

    • Cisplatin: This is used to treat many cancers including head and neck, cervical, lung, bladder, oesophageal and testicular cancers. It's usually given alongside radiation. It comes from the platinum class, along with carboplatin and oxaliplatin. It has a high risk of causing nausea and vomiting. Doctors usually give fluids through a vein to help protect the kidneys during treatment[16].

Paclitaxel: This belongs to the taxane group and is used for treating breast, ovarian, lung and head and neck cancers. It is given with a steroid and antihistamine as pre-medication to reduce the reactions during infusion. Any new tingling or numbness in the hands or feet should be reported promptly, as the doctors can adjust the doses[17].

  • Cyclophosphamide: This is an alkylating agent commonly used in treatment plans for breast cancer and lymphoma. When combined with certain other medicines, it can form a treatment combination with a high risk of nausea and vomiting[18].
  • Gemcitabine: This belongs to the antimetabolite group. Doctors use it to treat pancreatic, bladder, and lung cancers. Although it has a relatively low risk of nausea and vomiting, regular blood tests are important because it can affect blood cell counts[19].
  • Ondansetron: This is not chemotherapy, but an anti-sickness medication. It is a 5-HT3 antagonist, which is usually recommended by doctors to prevent vomiting. This makes chemotherapy easy for more patients to tolerate[20]. Fluorouracil, doxorubicin, etoposide, ifosfamide, and bleomycin are other common agents.

A note of caution: Never take a cancer medicine unless it is prescribed for you. Also, never stop/ adjust a dose yourself.

The medicines used: The chemotherapy medicines a doctor selects depend on the type of cancer, its stage, the goal of treatment and the patient's ability to handle the side effects. The same medicine may be used for different cancers, but the dose and combination of medicines can vary. This information is for general understanding only. Never buy or start these medicines without a qualified doctor's valid prescription.

Additional Information on Chemotherapy Side Effects

  • Preventing nausea and vomiting: The medicines used to prevent nausea, depend on how likely a chemotherapy regimen is to cause vomiting. For treatments with a high risk, doctors may prescribe a combination of medicines. Ask your doctor which combination is suitable for your treatment[20].
  • Managing fever during chemotherapy: Fever can indicate a serious infection when white blood cell levels are low[5]. In case of fever, head to the nearest hospital or check with your doctor immediately. Hospital teams assess the patient using blood tests, which may include blood cultures, to guide treatment. They may also check C-reactive protein (CRP), a marker of inflammation, to monitor the situation. However, CRP alone cannot confirm or rule out an infection[37].
  • Coping with hair loss: Scalp cooling during chemotherapy may help reduce hair loss, although its effectiveness varies[34]. A 2019 study in the British Journal of Cancer found that scalp cooling helped prevent hair loss in 43% of all patients included in the study and 54% of those who completed every treatment cycle[38]. Around 19% experienced headaches or felt cold during the procedure, but no serious side effects were reported[38]. However, some people may still experience significant hair loss despite using it.
  • Managing treatment-related fatigue: More than 80% of people receiving chemotherapy or radiotherapy experience fatigue. Gentle physical activity that gradually increases in intensity may help manage this tiredness better than avoiding activity altogether[35]. This guide on using exercise to cope with treatment-related fatigue, explains how to begin exercising, how often to be active and how much activity to aim for, even when treatment leaves you feeling very tired.
  • Protecting fertility: Discuss possible effects on fertility with your doctor before starting chemotherapy, as some treatments can affect the ability to have children[5]. For more information, visit side effects and management page, or cancer treatment hub.

If you need advice about a prescription or a symptom between hospital visits, you can arrange an online doctor consultation. However, suspected serious infections and other emergency symptoms require immediate in-person medical assessment.

What has changed recently

Medicines such as olanzapine are now commonly used alongside other anti-sickness medicines for chemotherapy treatments that have a high risk of causing vomiting. This combination helps control nausea and vomiting that may occur days after treatment[39]. Doctors may also prescribe growth factor injections to help the body produce more white blood cells, lowering the risk of infection[28]. In some people with early-stage, hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, tests that study the activity of genes in the tumour can help doctors decide whether chemotherapy is necessary[40].

Questions to ask your oncologist

  • Is the aim to cure the cancer, reduce the risk of it returning or keep it under control?
  • Which medicines will I receive, how many cycles will I need, and how long will the whole course take?
  • How likely is this treatment to cause vomiting, and should olanzapine be included?
  • When are my blood cell counts likely to be lowest, and which hospital should I visit if I develop a fever that night?
  • Will these medicines cause hair loss, and is scalp cooling available at this centre?
  • What is the total cost, along with medicines used to prevent or manage side effects?

What drives the cost

This section does not give prices in rupees. Costs depend on the city, hospital, cancer stage, treatment plan and whether care is provided by a public hospital, a charitable trust or a private hospital[41], [42]. Understanding how each part of treatment is charged can help you obtain an estimate.

The total cost depends on how treatment is charged: This may include the type of cancer, dose of mean, number of cycles, where it is given, and where you live[1]. Treatment expenses may continue for months or longer, depending on the medicine used and how well it works; some treatments do not have a fixed end date[46].

A study conducted at seven cancer centres in India found that 80.4% of patients receiving outpatient care faced healthcare costs that were difficult to manage financially. This highlights the importance of planning for treatment expenses, travel, medicines and other related costs[47].

Insurance:

Whether your management is covered depends on your policy, your insurer and the hospital rather than the treatment alone[48]. Four areas often cause confusion:

Day-care cover: Chemotherapy[7] and radiotherapy (external beam)[6] usually do not demand staying at hospital.[6], [7]

Waiting periods: Your policy may require you to wait before certain conditions or treatments are covered[48].

Room rent limits:[48] Exceeding the room-rent limit can lead to a proportionate reduction in the claim payment.

Pre-authorisation: Approval from the insurer for cashless treatment[48].

Frequently Asked Questions

How long will a single chemotherapy session take?

A single chemotherapy session may last from a few minutes to several hours, depending on the medicines used and how they are given. Some treatment regimens require longer infusions or medicines delivered over more than one day[49].

Will chemotherapy definitely cause hair loss?

Hair loss does not happen to everyone receiving chemotherapy, and its severity depends on the medicines and treatment combination used. Taxanes, such as paclitaxel and docetaxel, are strongly linked to hair loss. Platinum medicines, such as carboplatin, can also contribute to hair loss, especially when combined with taxanes. Ask your healthcare team about the risk of hair loss with your treatment plan and whether scalp cooling is an option[50].

Can I eat normally during chemotherapy?

Mostly yes. Food hygiene is especially important when your blood cell counts are low. Eat thoroughly cooked meat, fish, and eggs, and eat fruits except grapes that you can peel or wash well. You may need more protein and calories than usual, rather than less[51].

Can chemotherapy work even if I have no side effects?

Yes. chemotherapy can still work if even you are not having side effects. Also, having side effects doesn’t necessarily mean that your chemo is working[1], [5]. Doctors assess your response through examinations, blood tests and scans, rather than how unwell you feel.

Is oral chemotherapy weaker than injections?

No. Chemotherapy tablets can be as powerful as infusions and can also cause significant side effects and interactions[52]. They must be taken exactly as prescribed and stored safely, out of children’s reach.

Is it ok to work while on chemotherapy?

Many people continue working, particularly with less intensive treatment plans, and may reduce their hours during the week after each cycle[1]. So, discuss the likely pattern with your oncologist so that you can plan leave around the days when your blood cell counts are lowest.
