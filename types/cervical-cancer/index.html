Cervical cancer: causes, symptoms, stages and treatment

Medically reviewed by Dr Swetha Dama · Written by PharmEasy Medical Team · Published 6 November 2026 · Updated on 6 November 2026
Your pathSymptoms› Tests and diagnosis› Stages and types› Treatment › Medicines› Living with it

Cervical cancer is one of the few cancers that can be prevented. A common virus called human papillomavirus (HPV) causes it, and a vaccine can now protect against it. Even when HPV changes the cells of the cervix (the opening of the womb), these changes usually take many years to become cancer. This gives time for a screening test to find them, and for doctors to remove the abnormal tissue in a short procedure, usually without a hospital stay. This page will help you understand what cervical cancer is, how it is diagnosed, what to expect if further tests are recommended, and the various treatment options.

Even so, cervical cancer remains a serious health concern among women in India. It is the second most common cancer among Indian women, constituting upto 10.2% of all cancers in women. Cervical cancer accounted for 1,27,526 new cases and 79,906 deaths in India in 2022[1], [2], [3] About 1 in every 75 women are expected to develop cervical cancer at some point in their life[1], [3].

India has taken a major step towards prevention of cervical cancer on 28 February 2026 by launching a nationwide HPV vaccination programme at Ajmer, Rajasthan. A single dose of the quadrivalent HPV vaccine (Gardasil, covering types 6, 11, 16 and 18) is now offered free at government facilities every year to about 1.15 crore girls aged 14, with doses supplied through GAVI[4]. In a large Swedish study of the HPV vaccine, girls vaccinated before 17 years of age, had an 88% lower risk of cervical cancer compared to unvaccinated girls[5].

HPV: human papillomavirus; VIA: visual inspection with acetic acid

Why screening works: Most HPV infections clear on their own within one to two years. But when an infection with a high-risk type of HPV persists for years, it can lead to changes in the cells in transformation zone of the cervix. These early changes are called precancer or cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and are graded from mild to severe. Mild changes often resolve by themselves, but severe changes may eventually turn into cancer if left untreated. It usually takes about 5 to 10 years to develop precancer and around 15-20 years to develop cancer. The long gap is why the potential for effective screening exists. A simple test can diagnose early changes and cure with treatment, prior to development of cancer[6], [7], [14], [15].

The Indian picture

Two key features impact the current scenario in India. First, HPV types 16 and 18 account for a high proportion of cases. An estimated 83% of invasive cervical cancers in India are attributed to these two types, compared with the global figure of approximately 76% reported by the World Health Organization (WHO)[8], [30] Therefore, a vaccine that protects against HPV types 16 and 18 can address a larger proportion of cervical cancer cases in India.

The second key feature is the extent of screening coverage. India has a cervical cancer screening programme under the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD), implemented through Ayushman Arogya Mandirs. Under this programme, women aged 30-65 years are offered visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) at sub-health centres and primary health centres, with women who test positive referred for further assessment[13], [31]. In July 2025, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare reported that more than 10.18 crore women had been screened for cervical cancer[31]. However, screening coverage across the eligible population remains very low, and most Indian women visiting a cancer clinic have never undergone cervical cancer screening[32] .

Symptoms

Women with early cervical cancer and precancer usually experience no symptoms, which is exactly why screening exists. When symptoms do appear, the most common ones are[8], [33]:

  • Bleeding after intercourse (postcoital bleeding)
  • Bleeding between periods
  • Excessive or prolonged bleeding during periods
  • Any bleeding after menopause
  • Watery, blood-stained or foul-smelling vaginal discharge
  • Long-lasting pain in back, leg or hip region

Advanced disease can cause leg swelling, difficulties passing urine or stool, or blood in urine[33]. None of these symptoms is specific to cancer. Cervicitis, polyps, fibroids and hormonal irregularity often cause similar bleeding patterns[34], [35]. Any bleeding after intercourse or bleeding after menopause should be evaluated by a doctor[36], [37] You can read more on our cervical cancer symptoms page.

Causes and risk factors

Almost all cervical cancers are caused by persistent infection with a high-risk type of HPV. HPV is a common infection that spreads through intimate skin-to-skin contact. Most people who are sexually active will acquire it at some point in their lives, and most infection resolve on their own without symptoms[8], [14], [15].

Some factors increase the chance of HPV virus persistence in cervical cells. These include smoking, immunosuppression (e.g., HIV, being an organ transplant recipients), early age at first intercourse, long-term use of hormonal contraceptives, multiple full-term pregnancies, multiple sexual partners and the presence of other sexually transmitted infections[8], [14], [15]. Women from lower socio-economic strata are at a higher risk, partly because they are less likely to have access to regular screening and treatment for precancer[14], [38]. The causes and risk factors page covers each of these in detail, including what the evidence does and does not support.

Diagnosis of precancer and cancer

The following tests may be done to reach a diagnosis[11]:

  • Speculum examination: To visualise the cervix and take samples when required.
  • Colposcopy: An outpatient procedure performed in event of abnormal test (Pap smear/ HPV test) results. It involves closely examining the cervix and the walls of the vagina with the help of magnification.
  • Biopsy: Various types of biopsies based on the requirement are:
  • Colposcopy-directed biopsy: Using punch biopsy forceps, a small section of the abnormal area is sampled.
  • Other types of biopsies: Endocervical curettage sample is taken in all cases with HPV DNA positive test, either during speculum examination or colposcopy. It includes the insertion of a narrow instrument into the cervix to scrape some tissue for laboratory testing. Conization or cone biopsy is another procedure where a cone-shaped tissue is cut for a larger specimen for evaluation. Common methods of cone biopsies are [loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP)/large loop excision of the transformation zone (LLETZ)] or cold knife cone biopsy, which provides the full thickness of the cervix needed to measure depth of invasion.

If a biopsy confirms the presence of cancer cells, the doctor may recommend additional tests to determine whether the cancer has spread and, if so, the extent of its spread. Not all patients require every test described below. The choice of investigations depends on the findings of the physical examination and biopsy results[11].

  • Imaging: Various imaging techniques including computed tomography (CT) scan, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET)/CT scan are used. An MRI scan of the pelvis shows how far the tumour has spread around the cervix and into the nearby tissues[11], [39]. A PET-CT scan helps to assess spread anywhere in the whole body[11].
  • HIV testing: Given the association with immunosuppression, this test should be done[11].

The diagnosis and Pap smear page explains what each test involves and how to read the report.

Stages

The staging of cervical cancer is done using the FIGO 2018 system and its revision in 2019 as follows:

  • Stage I: The cancer is in the cervix. The cancer is confined to the cervix, the lower part of the uterus. Early cancers may be very small and visible only under a microscope. Includes stages IA and IB.
  • Stage II: Cancer has spread beyond the cervix. It may spread to the upper part of the vagina or the tissues surrounding the cervix but has not reached the pelvic wall or lower third of the vagina. Includes stages IIA and IIB.
  • Stage III: Cancer has more extensive local spread. It may reach the lower part of the vagina, the pelvic wall, or the nearby lymph nodes. It can also obstruct the urinary tract and affect kidney function. Includes stage IIIA, IIIB and IIIC.
  • Stage IV: The cancer reaches other organs. It may invade nearby organs such as the bladder or rectum or spread to distant organs such as the lungs or liver. Includes IVA (nearby organs) and IVB (distant spread)[40], [41].

Treatment

Cervical pre cancer and cancer treatment at each stage includes the following:

  • Precancer is treated with LEEP/LLETZ, cone biopsy, or thermal ablation or cryotherapy. The WHO offers a single-visit “screen-and-treat” approach[8].
  • Early-stage disease (stage 1) is usually treated with surgery. For selected stage IA1 cancers, a cone biopsy may be preferred for a woman who wants to have children with further observation advised if the edges of the cone do not have cancer cells. If the edges have cancer cells, another cone biopsy or radical trachelectomy may be advisable[12].
  • For low-risk tumours up to 2 cm with limited invasion, the SHAPE trial (2024) showed simple uterus removal may be done[42]. When radical hysterectomy is needed, it should be done by open surgery, as the LACC trial showed laparoscopic and robotic radical hysterectomy had worse survival rates and higher recurrence rates[43].
  • Locally advanced disease (IB3, IIA2, IIB to IVA) is treated with concurrent chemoradiation, in which external beam radiotherapy with weekly cisplatin, followed by brachytherapy (internal radiotherapy) is given[12], [44], [45]. Brachytherapy is an essential part of treatment; omitting it is linked to lower survival[45]. For high-risk stage III-IVA disease, adding pembrolizumab to chemoradiation improved survival in the KEYNOTE-A18 trial[46].
  • Metastatic (disease that has spread to other parts of the body) or recurrent disease whose tumour tests positive for the protein programmed death-ligand 1 may be treated with pembrolizumab plus platinum chemotherapy and paclitaxel, with or without bevacizumab, based on KEYNOTE-826[47], [48]. In the US, tisotumab vedotin received Food and Drug Administration approval in April 2024 for disease that has progressed on or after chemotherapy[49], [50].

Prevention and screening

Vaccination. A single dose of quadrivalent HPV vaccine is given free of charge to 14-year-old girls under India's national programme[58]. WHO’s Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization endorsed single-dose schedules in April 2022 following a 10-year follow-up of approximately 17,000 females in India. An International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) study demonstrated that one dose of the quadrivalent vaccine against persistent infections of HPV 16 and 18 provided protection comparable to two or three vaccines[59], [60]. Cervavac, the quadrivalent vaccine made by Serum Institute of India, uses a two-dose schedule and has received WHO-prequalification in 2026[61]. The questions parents ask most often are covered in: who the cervical cancer vaccine is for, the age limits and its side effects, and what the HPV vaccine is and when it should be taken.

Screening. For general population (from age 30 onwards), WHO recommends high-performance HPV testing at intervals of 5 to 10 years, and for women living with HIV, from age 25, at intervals of 3-5 years. Screening programme approved by Govt of India includes VIA for women aged 30-65 years. Self-collected samples have proved to be of equal efficacy in screening of cervical cancer. Any abnormality detected in the result requires consultation with health care provider. As current vaccine does not cover each type of cancer, screening is essential even after vaccination, as per the same schedule mentioned above[62],63.

General women's health panels are also available at private institutions outside the public programme: a cancer screening package for women aimed at women with no symptoms who want a baseline set of investigations, and a broader advanced women's cancer screening package for those whose doctor wants a wider panel. https\://pharmeasy.in/diagnostics/tests/ca-125-for-women-36The cervix is screened by HPV testing, VIA, or cytology and not a a blood sample[62], [64].

Related cancers

The HPV virus that causes cervical cancer can also cause cancer in other parts of the body, including the vagina, vulva, anus and back of the throat (oropharynx). You can read more on our pages on vaginal and vulvar cancer, anal cancer and HPV and cancer prevention.

Cervical cancer is different from uterine (endometrial) cancer and ovarian cancer that arise from different parts of the reproductive system and behave differently. To explore other cancers, see our full list of cancer types.

Insurance

Whether your treatment is covered depends on your policy, your insurer and the hospital, not on the treatment itself. Four things that often people are unaware of[70], [71], [72], [73]:

  • Day-care cover: Chemotherapy and radiotherapy usually don’t need an overnight stay, so check that your policy covers day-care treatment.
  • Waiting periods: Check the waiting period for pre-existing conditions, which is now capped at three years.
  • Room-rent caps: Check the room-rent limit, as choosing a room above this limit can proportionally reduce your entire claim, not just the room charges.
  • Pre-authorisation for cashless claims: Check the insurer’s timelines, as cashless requests must be decided within one hour and discharge authorisation granted within three hours.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does having HPV mean I have cancer?

No. HPV infection is very common, and most infections clear on their own within a year or two. Only a long-lasting infection with a high-risk type, over several years, can lead to precancer. A positive HPV test means you need follow-up tests, not that you have cancer[11], [15].

Does a positive HPV test mean my partner was unfaithful?

No. HPV can stay inactive in the body for many years, so a positive test cannot tell you when or from whom the infection came. It does not mean your partner has been unfaithful[74].

I have had the HPV vaccine. Do I still need screening?

Yes. The vaccine protects against the most common cancer-causing types of HPV but not all of them, and it does not clear an infection you already have. Screening from age 30 (age 25 for women living with HIV) still applies[8], [14].

Is cervical cancer curable?

Often, yes. Early cervical cancer can be cured, usually with surgery. Larger tumours or cancers that have spread to nearby lymph nodes are also treated with the aim to cure, using chemoradiation with brachytherapy. Cancer that has spread to distant organs, or has come back after treatment, usually cannot be cured, but treatment can control it and relieve symptoms. Early and locally advanced cervical cancers are both treated with the intent to cure by offering surgery for small tumours and chemoradiation with brachytherapy for larger and node-positive disease. Advanced and recurrent disease is treated for control of cancer and relief of symptoms[8], [12].

Can I still have children after treatment?

Sometimes. In selected early cases, cone biopsy or radical trachelectomy can keep the womb so a future pregnancy is possible. Chemoradiation usually causes infertility and early menopause, but options such as moving the ovaries out of the radiation field (ovarian transposition) or freezing eggs may help. These must be discussed before treatment begins, not after[12], [75].

Is cervical cancer hereditary?

Cervical cancer is caused by a virus, not by a single inherited gene. However, having a mother or sister with cervical cancer or a history of cervical does increase the risk somewhat. This makes regular screening especially important[39].
