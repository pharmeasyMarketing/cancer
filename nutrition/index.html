Nutrition and diet during cancer treatment

Medically reviewed by Dr. Sarthak Moharir · Written by PharmEasy Medical Team · Published 6 November 2026 · Updated on 6 November 2026

Commonly cancer patients tend to eat light and simple meals but it’s important to understand that during cancer treatment, the body often needs more nourishment and not less. Cancer and its treatment can increase the body’s demand, at the same time making eating difficult due to several reasons.

A nutritious diet is particularly important because malnutrition affects an estimated 20% to 70% of people with cancer. This may lead to complications after surgery or longer hospital stays and poorer tolerance to treatment[1]. Poor nutritional status can also contribute to chemotherapy dose reductions, treatment delays, or early discontinuation, turning a nutrition problem into a treatment problem[2]. Also, sarcopenia (the loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength) can commonly occur in older adults with cancer, which is associated with poorer outcomes[3]. Therefore adequate energy and protein are essential to help meet the body’s needs, maintain adequate weight and preserve muscle mass[4].

It is also important to note that nutritional needs vary depending on the type of cancer and its treatment. Someone receiving chemotherapy for breast cancer may experience nausea or changes in appetite, while a person undergoing radiotherapy to the mouth and throat may struggle with swallowing[5], [6]. In pancreatic cancer, reduced digestive enzyme production can make it difficult to digest food and absorb nutrients.7 So, nutritional goals have to be set accordingly.

This page focuses on the nutritional principles that apply across different types of cancer, while recognising that individual needs vary depending on the cancer, its treatment, and the side effects a patient experiences.

What the body actually needs during treatment

Why intake falls just as demand rises: A few things change during cancer treatment; the metabolic impact of cancer and its treatment can increase energy demands of the body. Moreover, side effects like tissue damage/repair and muscle breakdown make adequate protein intake especially important[8], [9]. So the body may need more energy and protein but at the same time, nausea, changes in taste, mouth pain, feeling full quickly, fatigue, and low mood can make it harder to eat enough[10]. And when the body needs more nourishment but receives less maintaining weight and muscle mass becomes challenging. One practical approach is to eat smaller and more frequent meals while choosing energy- and protein-rich foods rather than relying on three large meals a day.

  • Protein sources that work in Indian kitchens: Good sources of protein in Indian diets include dals, rajma, chana, paneer, curd, milk, eggs, chicken, fish, soya chunks, tofu, groundnuts, seeds, and besan[11]. In many vegetarian households the issue is not a lack of protein rich foods but the amount consumed (because rice or roti makes up most of the meal). So a simple change is to increase the proportion of dal relative to rice or roti which can help add more protein without making drastic changes to familiar eating habits.
  • Adding energy without adding volume: Simple additions can make everyday meals more nourishing without increasing portion sizes too much.
    • A little ghee or oil can be added to dal, khichdi, or vegetables.
    • Milk can be used instead of water to prepare porridge, ragi, or sattu. Powdered nuts can also be mixed into milk.
    • Adding paneer to sabzi and choosing full-fat milk instead of toned milk can also help increase energy intake.
  • Do not set numeric targets from a web page: Protein and energy needs greatly vary depending on body weight, cancer type, treatment, and kidney function[12]. A dietitian at your cancer centre can help determine how much you need and how to meet those needs during treatment. If you have not been referred to one ask your oncology team for a referral.

Eating through taste change, mouth sores and nausea

These three problems are among the common reasons patients struggle to eat during cancer treatment. What a patient should know is that each has different causes and management strategies.

1. Managing taste change

Taste changes during cancer treatment are often overlooked and mistaken for a loss of appetite. What actually happens in reality is that patients may still feel hungry but find that food tastes different or unpleasant. So identifying how taste has shifted can make it easier to find foods that are still enjoyable.

2. Mouth sores and difficulty swallowing

A common side effect that can occur with certain chemotherapy drugs and radiotherapy to the head and neck is mucositis. It is the inflammation and ulceration of the lining of the mouth and throat, making eating, drinking, and swallowing painful[15]. When the pain becomes severe, patients may struggle to eat enough to meet their nutritional needs. So, managing mucositis is an important part of maintaining nutrition during treatment.

The National Cancer Institute (NCI) recommends avoiding foods that can irritate the mouth. These include citrus fruits, spicy foods, tomatoes, ketchup, salty foods, and foods with rough or sharp textures[16]. Avoiding these in Indian cooking can be difficult because they are important in many recipes. However, there are some practical substitutions you can make:

  • Replace tomato-based gravies with curd, coconut milk, cashew paste or plain onion bases
  • Replace tamarind or lime souring with a little curd.
  • Use white pepper (or none at all) instead of chilli.
  • Use soft foods like curd rice, khichdi with ghee, idli soaked in warm dal, upma, ragi porridge, custard, shrikhand, banana with milk, well-cooked halwa. Another option is to blend your food. Blending makes them easier to swallow when mouth pain is severe.
  • Cold foods like lassi, milkshakes, kulfi, cold curd, chilled coconut water can also provide temporary relief.

A dental checkup before treatment is an important step for patients scheduled for radiotherapy to the head and neck or intensive chemotherapy[17]. Once treatment starts you should check the mouth daily for redness, sores, or ulcers. Rinsing regularly with a solution of warm water, salt, and baking soda may help keep the mouth clean. Additionally, brush gently with a soft-bristled toothbrush after meals and before bed to maintain oral hygiene while minimising irritation[13].

Call your doctor if mouth, lip or throat pain is preventing eating, drinking or sleeping, or if there is fever of 100.5°F/38°C or above. More is on the side effects page.

3. Nausea, vomiting and appetite loss

Nausea during cancer treatment is best managed with medication first and then followed by dietary adjustments. Antiemetics (medicines that prevent or relieve nausea and vomiting) generally work better when taken as prescribed, before symptoms begin, rather than after vomiting starts[18]. So even on days when you feel well follow your prescribed medication schedule unless your oncology team advises otherwise. Keep in mind that skipping preventive medication can make nausea and vomiting harder to control during subsequent treatment cycles.

Now comes the dietary adjustment part that works alongside medicines:

  • Try eating small and frequent meals instead of three large ones.
  • Choose bland foods such as toast, rice, plain khichdi, or crackers.
  • Sip fluids regularly throughout the day rather than drinking large amounts at once.
  • Avoid greasy or heavily spiced foods.
  • Try eating in a calm, well-ventilated space away from cooking smells (which can trigger nausea).
  • Staying upright for about 30 minutes after meals may also help reduce discomfort.

If nausea begins even before you reach the hospital or start treatment it may be anticipatory nausea. This can develop when the brain begins to associate the treatment setting with nausea and vomiting experienced during earlier cycles[19]. Many things like relaxation techniques, guided imagery, and distraction may help manage these symptoms. These options could sometimes be considered alongside medication prescribed by the oncology team. The best way to reduce the risk is to control nausea effectively from the very first treatment cycle, before these associations develop.

Appetite loss without nausea is also common, but you should still try eating at regular intervals rather than waiting until you feel hungry. If this happens:

  • Take smaller meals after every 2-3 hours instead of 3 large meals.
  • Keep energy- and protein-rich snacks within easy reach so you can eat whenever you feel up to it.
  • Opt for smaller plates as when the appetite is low, a huge one could feel overwhelming.
  • Try having your largest meal when your appetite is best (morning or whenever you feel).

The medicines used: The oncology team will decide whether medication is needed based on what is affecting the patient's appetite, the treatment being given, and kidney and liver function. Remember that these medicines are intended to help manage symptoms and not treat the cancer itself. They should not be taken without a prescription and guidance from a doctor.

Food safety when white cell counts are low

Chemotherapy reduces neutrophil cell counts. This weakens the body’s ability to protect against foodborne infections during this period. Safe food handling and hygiene can help in such cases[24], [25].

Some practical rules for an Indian kitchen[26], [27]:

  • Cook meat, fish and eggs thoroughly and avoid runny egg yolks or undercooked meat.
  • Wash fruit and vegetables thoroughly under running water or choose fruit that you can peel yourself.
  • Avoid unpasteurised milk and products made from it. Make sure to boil milk that is brought from loose sources.
  • Avoid street food, fruit cut sold in the open, roadside juices, chutneys kept at room temperature and buffet meals.
  • Use separate chopping boards and utensils for raw meat and fruit or vegetables, and wash your hands with soap before preparing or eating food.
  • Eat freshly cooked food, refrigerate leftovers promptly, reheat thoroughly, and throw away anything you are unsure is safe.

Note: A fever of 100.5°F/38°C or higher during this period needs medical attention rather than waiting to see whether it improves. Do not take paracetamol to lower the fever before contacting your doctor, as it can hide signs of a more serious problem[24].

Cachexia: when eating more is not enough

Cancer cachexia is a complex condition involving the ongoing loss of muscle mass. It is important to understand that it cannot be fully reversed through ordinary nutritional support alone. This is because it is caused by inflammation and changes in the body's metabolism and not simply by eating too little[33].

Explaining this clearly is important because families may see continued weight loss as a sign that they have not fed the patient properly, while the patient may feel that they have not tried hard enough. In this situation neither is to blame for the changes caused by cachexia. Providing food remains important because it supports strength, daily functioning and the ability to cope with treatment. However, along with this understanding that food alone cannot reverse cachexia is also equally important. Cachexia needs clinical care, including palliative care when appropriate, as discussed on the living with cancer page.

Blood tests have a useful but limited role. An inflammatory marker such as C-reactive protein (CRP) can show the level of inflammation associated with cachexia[34]. A thyroid profile may sometimes be checked when weight appetite or energy are not improved by treatment[35]. It is important to understand that neither test measures nutrition, nor does it confirm or rule out cancer. These tests are requested to help explain a change rather than establish a diagnosis.

Anti-cancer diets, alkaline diets and what they cost

People often spend money and place their hopes in special diets soon after diagnosis when they are looking for something they can do. Therefore, these concerns deserve a clear and thoughtful answer.

  • The alkaline diet. Some people believe that cancer cannot survive in an alkaline environment and that changing the diet can make the body more alkaline[36]. However, the human body keeps blood pH within a very narrow range and food does not change blood pH[37], [38]. Eating more vegetables and fewer processed foods is a reasonable recommendation, but the claim that this treats cancer is unsupported. Actually, no dietary pattern has been shown to treat cancer[39] and the money often charged for that promise is not justified.
  • Juicing and detox regimens. These replace solid food with juice[16]. But they have low fiber content and are high in sugars[40]. There is no established process by which a juice or detox regimen removes cancer from the body. Hence, falling for such claims is irrational.
  • Ketogenic and extreme low-carbohydrate diets. Eating sugar has not been shown to make cancer worse. On the other hand, restricting carbohydrates is not a standard approach to cancer management and may make it harder for patients to meet their energy needs during treatment[41], [42]. Also, restrictive diets during cancer management may lead to nutrient deficiency and weight loss.
  • Diet with fruits. Some plant extracts may destroy cancer cells in specific laboratory environments[43]. Such laboratory findings are an early step in developing medicines, rather than a proof that eating the fruit treats cancer..
  • Herbal and alternative remedies. No herbal product has been shown to treat cancer effectively. Instead some products can interfere with how chemotherapy medicines work or affect liver function, potentially making treatment less effective or increasing its harmful effects[41], [44]. Tell your oncologist about everything you take, including Ayurvedic and homeopathic preparations. This is to check for possible interactions, rather than judge your choices.
  • Supplements.There is no evidence that supplements like L-carnitine reduce cancer-related fatigue[45]. High-dose antioxidant supplements are also generally discouraged during radiotherapy or chemotherapy[44]. If blood tests suggest certain deficiencies (like iron, vitamin B12 and vitamin D deficiencies) it should be corrected on medical advice rather than taking supplements without an identified need.

Important: Nutrition supports cancer treatment, but it does not replace it. No dietary pattern has been shown to treat cancer. For more details, check effective ways to reduce your cancer risk.

When to see a doctor

Contact your team the same day if

  • You have a fever of 100.5°F/38°C or higher[24].
  • You cannot keep fluids down for more than 12 hours.
  • You cannot swallow your medicines.
  • You have severe or bloody diarrhea.
  • You develop signs such as passing very little urine, feeling dizzy when standing or becoming confused[46].
  • Mouth pain stops you from eating, drinking or sleeping[47].

Arrange an appointment within a week or two if

  • You have lost more significant body weight since treatment began[2].
  • Your appetite has reduced and you cannot enjoy food.
  • Your stools have become pale, greasy or unpleasant-smelling[30].
  • You have constipation or diarrhoea that is not going away[46].
  • You are struggling with eating and have not been referred to a dietitian.

One study at a single institution reported longer overall survival when nutritional counselling was included in care from a team of different specialists, at 14.8 months compared against 11.9 months. The finding needs confirmation in other settings, but it supports seeking nutritional advice rather than managing without it[25].

If travelling to your cancer center between treatment cycles is difficult, an online doctor consultation can help you obtain medical advice about symptoms, medicines for nausea or digestion, and whether you need to be examined in person.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I stop eating sugar if I have cancer?

Cancer cells use more glucose than normal cells, but studies have not shown that eating sugar makes cancer worse or that avoiding it slows the disease. So, restricting carbohydrates during treatment can increase the risk of losing weight and muscle when maintaining both is especially important[41], [42].

Are there fruits that kill cancer cells?

No fruit has been shown to treat cancer. Plant substances that kill cancer cells in a laboratory do not necessarily have the same effect when a person eats them in ordinary food amounts[43]. So you should eat fruits as part of good nutrition rather than as a cancer treatment.

Does an alkaline diet help cancer?

The lungs and kidneys closely control blood pH, and food does not change it[38]. Eating more vegetables is reasonable, but the claim that an alkaline diet treats cancer is not supported by evidence.

Can I eat non-vegetarian food during chemotherapy?

Meat, fish and eggs can provide useful protein during treatment[25]. You should make sure to cook them thoroughly, avoid undercooked meat, rare meat or runny eggs. Also, avoid meat and fish from unhygienic sources when your white cell counts are low[26].

Should I take protein powder?

Discuss it with your dietitian before buying it yourself. Oral nutritional supplements can help when you cannot get enough nutrition from food[25]. However the suitable product and amount depend on your kidney function and what you already eat.

Is it safe to fast during cancer treatment?

Discuss religious or ritual fasting with your oncology team first. Fasting can affect medicine schedules, hydration and blood sugar levels[48]. So prolonged fasting is not advisable when you are already losing weight..

My relative refuses to eat. What should I do?

Offer small amounts of food regularly instead of large meals, and make each meal mouthful and rich in energy. Keep mealtimes free from pressure. Then consult your doctor to check for treatable causes, such as nausea, mouth pain, constipation, depression or any digestive problem. Remember forcing someone to eat is unlikely to help and may add to their distress[16], [25].
