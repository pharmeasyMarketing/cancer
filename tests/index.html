Cancer tests, biopsy and tumour markers

Medically reviewed by Dr Suvarna Kulkarni · Written by PharmEasy Medical Team · Published 6 November 2026 · Updated on 6 November 2026

Cancer is a group of diseases in which abnormal cells grow uncontrollably and may invade nearby tissues or spread to other parts of the body[1]. Several tests such as an ultrasound, computed tomography (CT) scan, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scan, endoscopy, and blood tests, including tumour markers, are considered during the diagnosis. These tests generally provide information that helps the doctor assess whether cancer may be present and determine its extent. In most cases, a biopsy, in which a sample of the suspected tissue is checked under a microscope, is used to confirm the diagnosis[2].

Knowing this difference makes cancer testing easier to understand. A scan may show an abnormality, but it may not always be clear exactly what the abnormality is[2]. Similarly, increased tumour marker levels cannot confirm a cancer diagnosis[3], and normal blood test results cannot always rule out cancer,2 especially when symptoms persist. The National Cancer Institute (NCI) states that tumour marker tests alone cannot be used for cancer screening, as they may miss people who do have the disease and flag people who do not[3].

In India, guidelines recommend confirming the cancer before initiating treatment. The National Cancer Grid advises doctors to confirm the condition through histopathology (a biopsy) before starting any cancer-related treatment. For breast lumps, doctors are advised to perform a triple assessment, as follows:

* A physical examination (ideally, by a breast surgeon)

* Bilateral imaging test that includes a bilateral mammogram (a special x-ray of both breasts) and/or ultrasound or MRI

* A histopathologic test with a biopsy or fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), in which a tissue from the suspected portion is extracted and checked under a microscope.

All three should be performed rather than relying on any one[4].

What can trigger investigation?

Cancer testing can be done for several reasons. One might have some symptoms that persist, recur, or worsen[5]; a screening result that shows an abnormal finding[6]; or a scan done for another health condition shows an unexpected abnormal result. Incidental findings of health issues are becoming more common as imaging tests become more affordable. However, many may turn out to be harmless, while some might require further testing[7].

When a person has persistent or worsening symptoms, the doctor will usually ask about their medical history and perform a physical examination. This helps the doctor decide which tests are needed, such as imaging tests, blood tests, or biopsy[2]. If you are unsure which symptoms may be linked to different cancers, you can go through our overview of the main types of cancer and the early signs, before discussing any screening tests with your doctor.

Biopsy: what it is and how it is done?

A biopsy refers to the collection of cells or tissue from the suspected area, which is then sent to a pathologist. The pathologist looks at the sample under a microscope to check for cancer cells. The type of biopsy depends on where the abnormal area is located and the procedure needed to collect the sample.

There are several types of biopsies. Some biopsies require only a small sample of cells from the body that can be collected using a tiny needle. Others require a larger piece of tissue/organ. In some cases, a doctor may use a camera or imaging tests to help them locate the area of biopsy[8].

Can a biopsy spread cancer?

The risk that cancer cells will spread along the path of a biopsy needle is extremely low. The American Cancer Society explains that the benefits of obtaining an accurate diagnosis generally outweigh this small risk[15]. Patients considering a biopsy should discuss this procedure with their doctor in order to make t decision on an individual basis. A biopsy is generally done by a surgeon or a radiologist trained in oncology so that spread due to biopsy is practically reduced to nil

5. Blood tests and tumour markers

Blood tests can provide useful clues about a person’s health, but no single blood test can confirm or rule out cancer.

Blood tests that are not tumour markers

  • A Complete Blood Count (CBC): This test measures the number and size of different types of blood cells. It cannot directly diagnose cancer; however, it can help in the diagnosis of some cancer types by identifying patterns[2]. For example, a very high white blood cell (WBC) count with a lot of abnormal immature cells could be a sign of leukaemia[23].
  • Other Blood Tests (such as liver and kidney function tests, calcium, albumin and coagulation tests): These can provide information about how well the organs are working and may help doctors assess a person's overall health before treatment. Changes in some of these test results may also provide clues about certain cancers[24].
  • Stool Occult Blood Test: This test differs from a blood test since it analyses stool samples for traces of blood. A positive result does not automatically mean cancer. Conditions such as piles, anal fissures, ulcers, and some medicines can also cause blood in the stool. A positive result may lead to further testing, such as a colonoscopy[25].
  • C-Reactive Protein Test (CRP): This test measures CRP levels. CRP levels increase in response to inflammation in the body that may be caused by infection, arthritis, injury, or a recent surgery[26]. The test cannot screen for, diagnose or exclude cancer. Our guide on what a CRP result does and does not mean for cancer explains why a high CRP level does not necessarily mean a person has cancer.
  • Antinuclear Antibody Test (ANA): This is not a tumour marker. The test is usually not present in the cancer panel list. It's mainly used for the assessment of autoimmune disease rather than to diagnose cancer. However, sometimes certain types of cancer can still produce ANAs. However, this test alone cannot confirm the condition[27]. use tumour marker tests alongside imaging scans, biopsies and other investigations to help assess cancer.

Tumour markers, one by one

Doctors use tumour marker tests alongside imaging scans, biopsies and other tests to help identify cancer. A tumour marker is a substance, usually a protein, that some cancer cells release. However, healthy cells and tissues affected by inflammation can also produce these substances[28]. Doctors usually get more useful information by comparing marker levels over time. For example, a decrease after treatment may indicate that the treatment is working, while a steady increase may suggest that the cancer has returned. However, tumour marker results alone cannot confirm whether cancer is present or has come back[29]. Normal ranges and measurement units can vary between laboratories and testing methods. Therefore, always use the reference range/values given in your own results.

The following section explains how doctors use different tumour markers and what their results may mean, in detail.

  • Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP): This is useful in certain liver cancers and germ cell tumours. However, high AFP levels can also occur in non-cancerous liver conditions, such as hepatitis[31]. They may also rise when certain cancers ( such as colorectal cancer) spread to the liver[30]. Therefore, doctors interpret the results alongside other findings[31], as normal AFP does not rule out hepatocellular cancer, particularly in an early tumour.
  • In testicular cancer, AFP has an important role in interpreting the tumour type. Pure seminoma (the type of testicular cancer that has only seminoma cells, with no germ cells) does not produce AFP. Therefore, if AFP is high when a tumour has been reported as pure seminoma, doctors consider whether a non-seminomatous component may also be present[30]. You can go through the alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) page for more information.
  • Beta Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (Beta-hCG): This is best known as a pregnancy hormone, but some germ cell tumours and gestational trophoblastic diseases can also produce it. Its level is, therefore, interpreted in the context of the patient's age, sex, pregnancy status, and suspected condition[42]. Its levels are high in about 14% of stage I pure seminomas, around 50% of seminomas that have spread, and 40-60% of non-seminomas[43]. In germ cell tumours, beta-hCG is used alongside AFP and lactate dehydrogenase levels to help assess the cancer and classify patients into risk groups. Together, this classification helps doctors estimate the likely outcome and plan treatment[42]. Increased beta-hCG level is, therefore, not an indicator of cancer by itelf, since pregnancy and other factors might influence it[33]. To know more about this test, please go through beta-hCG total.
  • Cancer Antigen-125 (CA-125): This laboratory test is primarily used to monitor epithelial ovarian cancer. Interpreting the test results is complicated because several non-cancerous conditions can cause high CA-125 levels, while its levels may be normal in many women with early-stage ovarian cancer. Therefore, doctors do not rely only on the outcome of this analysis to determine whether a patient has ovarian cancer[34]. Check CA-125 for women to know more.
  • Cancer Antigen- 15-3 (CA 15-3): This test helps monitor treatment response in metastatic breast cancer where levels of this tumour marker were initially high. It is not useful for diagnosing, screening, or treatment planning for breast cancer on its own. Its levels can also increase in non-cancerous breast disease[36]. Check CA 15-3 to know more.
  • Cancer Antigen- 19-9 (CA 19-9): This is associated with several types of cancers such as pancreatic, gallbladder, bile duct, and stomach. It also helps assess and monitor certain types of treatment. Several non-cancerous conditions, such as gallstones, cirrhosis, pancreatitis and bile duct blockage or infection, can also increase the values of CA 19-9. Another limitation is genetic: about 5-10% of people are Lewis antigen-negative and cannot produce CA 19-9. Their CA 19-9 can, therefore, remain low even when cancer is present[39]. Check CA 19-9 for more information.
  • Beta-2 microglobulin: This is a protein found on the surface of most cells. Its levels may indicate the severity of cancers such as multiple myeloma, certain types of lymphoma and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia. However, this marker is not specific and it alone cannot indicate or rule out a cancer. Its levels may also increase with kidney problems and chronic infections including HIV and inflammation. Therefore, it is usually interpreted along with tests such as a kidney function tests[40]. Read beta-2 microglobulin for more information.
  • Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA): This is mainly used to assess the risk of colorectal cancer[41]. According to NCG India guidance, doctors may check CEA along with a clinical examination every 3-6 months during the first five years of follow-up after cancer treatment[44]. If CEA levels return to normal after treatment but begin to rise again, it may indicate that the cancer has returned[29]. A CEA level above 100 ng/mL usually indicates metastatic disease, which requires a thorough investigation[41]. CEA normal levels can vary depending on the testing method and laboratory, so always check the reference range given in your test report. Smoking and certain health conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, pancreatitis, cirrhosis, hypothyroidism, and peptic ulcer can also increase CEA levels[29]. Please check the CEA page to know more about this marker.
  • Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA): This marker helps evaluate the risk of prostate cancer. It is produced by both normal prostate tissue and cancer tissue. A PSA value of 4.0 ng/mL is traditionally considered high enough to warrant further tests for prostate cancer. However, no single PSA level can confirm or rule out prostate cancer. Among men with high PSA levels who undergo a biopsy, only about 25% of men are found to have prostate cancer. Several non-cancerous conditions, such as an enlarged prostate, prostatitis, and urinary infection, can also increase PSA. A recent biopsy, strenuous activities, and recent ejaculation can also affect PSA value. Therefore, doctors consider PSA levels along with symptoms, medical history and other test results to decide whether further tests are needed. In some cases, doctors also give medicines to shrink the prostate and lower the PSA values[28]. Know more about PSA for men.
  • Thyroglobulin: This blood test monitors certain types of thyroid cancers after treatment. Thyroglobulin is normally produced by an organ called the thyroid in small amounts. If thyroglobulin levels rise during follow-up, it may indicate the return of cancer or that some thyroid tissue remains even after treatment. However, the result alone cannot confirm cancer recurrence. Doctors also consider anti-thyroglobulin antibodies, as these can affect the accuracy of the test[45]. A thyroid lump cannot be confirmed as cancer through a thyroglobulin (Tg) blood test alone. Further assessment, including a needle biopsy when needed, may be necessary. People always get confused between a thyroid profile (T3, T4, TSH) and a Tg test. A thyroid profile is not a cancer test by itself, it shows how well the thyroid gland is working. However, it helps monitor hormone levels after thyroid surgery or neck radiation for thyroid cancer, as patients may need hormone replacement.

What a pathology report actually says

A histopathology report presents the findings from a thorough examination of the tissue sample under a microscope. It helps identify the type of cancer and provides details that guide treatment[46].

The main details in a report may include:

  • Specimen and Site: What was removed and from where.
  • Histological Type: The specific type of cancer, for example, invasive ductal carcinoma.
  • Grade: How abnormal the cancer cells look.
  • Tumour Size and Extent: The tumour's size and how far it has spread into the nearby tissues. Example: millimetres, including depth.
  • Margins: Whether cancer cells are present at the edges of the removed tissue and how far they are from those edges[46].
  • Lymphovascular and Perineural Invasion: Whether cancer cells are present in small blood or lymph vessels or around nerves.
  • Lymph Nodes: The number of lymph nodes examined and how many contain cancer cells[46].
  • Immunohistochemistry[46]: Test results for proteins or hormones, such as HER2 and hormone receptors, help confirm the cancer type and guide treatment choices.
  • Molecular Results: Specific changes in the cancer cells that may help doctors determine specific treatments that are more likely to work.
  • Pathological TNM Stage (pTNM): The stage of the cancer based on surgical findings and tissue examination, including the tumour size, nearby lymph nodes, and distant spread[47].

How staging is decided and how long it takes

Cancer staging uses information from the physical examination, scans, and laboratory findings to assess how far the disease has progressed. Clinical stage (cTNM) is determined before treatment using examination findings and imaging tests. Pathological stage (pTNM) is established after surgery by examining the removed tissue. These two stages can sometimes be different.

Gynaecological cancers may be staged using the International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) system. Blood cancers, such as leukaemia and lymphoma, may follow different staging approaches because they do not always form solid tumours[47].

The process from the first consultation to a treatment plan may take one to three weeks, depending on how quickly scans and biopsies are completed. If additional molecular tests are needed, the results may take another week or two. These findings can help doctors select the most appropriate treatment for the patient.

When to see a doctor

Consult a doctor if you notice any of the following or have a test result you do not understand.

  • You should consult a doctor soon for the following symptoms: A lump that is persistent beyond three weeks, an unhealed mouth ulcer or patch, bleeding or numbness of lips, blood in urine or stool, difficulty swallowing, a changing mole or a new one, unexpected weight loss[48], or bleeding after menopause. You should also consult a doctor for any abnormal test result you do not understand.
  • Same Day of the Treatment is needed for the following: Heavy bleeding; fever above 38°C; nausea or vomiting; difficulty breathing during or after chemotherapy[49]; severe abdominal pain after a biopsy (for CT-guided pelvic biopsy)[50]; or a wound that feels hot, gets redder or more swollen, or leaks fluid or blood[50].
  • Do Not Rely Completely on a Tumour Marker Result: If your tumour marker levels increase again, and you do not have any symptoms, the right decision is to consult your doctor rather than going for a scan by yourself. An online doctor consultation can help you understand your result and whether further testing is necessary. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a biopsy test?

A biopsy involves taking a small sample of tissue or cells from the body for testing. A pathologist examines the tissue under a microscope and performs other tests if needed to check for cancer and identify its type. The findings may also help the doctor assess how aggressive the condition is[2].

Is a biopsy painful, and is it hazardous?

Most biopsies are performed only after a local anaesthetic is given (however, some may not even require it)[8]. It usually causes pressure instead of pain, with soreness for a day or two after the procedure. Bleeding and infection are not that common, but they still can occur[50]. There is very little evidence to suggest that a biopsy can spread cancer[15]. However, the risk of not undergoing a biopsy can be much bigger.

What is the full form of CEA?

The full form of CEA is carcinoembryonic antigen. It is a tumour marker which is primarily used for the diagnosis of colorectal cancer along with other tests. Doctors may also measure it before treatment to establish a baseline before surgery and to detect cancer recurrence. It may also be elevated in smokers and in several non-cancerous conditions[29].

Is there any single blood test which detects all the types of cancer?

No. There is no single test which can confirm/detect all types of cancer. Blood tests that aim to detect several cancers early are still being studied, but they are not yet approved for routine use[51]. Also, no available group of markers can confirm or rule out all types of cancer[3].

Which type of cancer causes a high WBC count?

An extremely high WBC count is usually caused by infection[52], severe inflammation, or steroids. However, it may also be caused by blood cancers, such as leukaemia, particularly when immature or abnormal cells appear on the blood smear, or when the increased WBC count is associated with anaemia[23] and low platelet levels.

My tumour marker result shows slightly high levels. Does that mean I have cancer?

Probably not. Some non-cancerous conditions may also cause high tumour marker levels. A slightly high result does not automatically mean you have cancer[28], [41]. Your doctor will consider your symptoms, medical history, and previous test results before deciding whether further tests are needed. For people already diagnosed with cancer, changes in marker levels over time may be more useful than a single result.

When will I get my biopsy report?

A biopsy result usually takes three to seven working days for the main report to be sent to you. Additional tests, such as immunohistochemistry, may take a few more days, while molecular testing can take one to three weeks. The results may arrive separately as each test is completed.
