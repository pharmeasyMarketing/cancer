HPV and cancer

Medically reviewed by Dr Swetha Dama · Written by PharmEasy Medical Team · Published 6 November 2026 · Updated on 6 November 2026

India now has a national HPV vaccination programme, which was launched on 28 February 2026 at Ajmer, Rajasthan. The programme will offer a single dose of quadrivalent HPV vaccine free of cost at government facilities every year to approximately 1.15 crore girls aged 14 years across all states and union territories[1]. In this programme, doses are supplied through Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance. The HPV vaccine has also been incorporated into India’s routine immunisation schedule[1], [2].

The programme would significantly reduce the burden of cervical cancer in India. Cervical Cancer is the second most common cancer among Indian women, accounting for 10% of all female cancers[3]. The Global Cancer Observatory estimates that India recorded 79,360 new cases and 36,319 deaths from cervical cancer in 2024[4].

Nearly all cases of cervical cancer are caused by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV). This makes cervical cancer one of the few common cancers that can be largely prevented through vaccination[5]. It is also important to note that HPV is not just a concern for women or cervical cancer. It can also cause cancers of the oropharynx, anus, vulva, vagina, penis and even head and neck cancers[6]. So, vaccination remains a pivotal step in avoiding cervical cancer and other related risks.

What the evidence shows

HPV is a group of more than 200 related viruses that infect the skin and mucosal surfaces (the inner linings of your body's cavities and organs)[7]. Among these around 30 to 40 types can infect the genital tract[8].

HPV spreads primarily through intimate skin-to-skin or mucosal contact during sexual activity even without penetrationv. This is why condoms can reduce the risk of HPV transmission but cannot eliminate it completely as the virus may infect areas of the skin not covered by condoms[10].

HPV types are classified as low-risk or high-risk based on their potential to cause disease.

  • Low-risk types 6 and 11 cause approximately 90% of genital warts[18].
  • High-risk types 16 and 18 are responsible for about 70% of cervical cancers worldwide. Other types 31, 33,35, 45,51,39, 52 and 58,56,59 account for a further 10-20% of cases[19].

How it changes risk

Most sexually active people acquire HPV at some point in their lives. In most cases an HPV infection clears naturally within one to two years without causing symptoms. Cancer develops in only a small proportion of people whose high-risk HPV infection persists for many years[7]. Some important points to keep in mind:

  • Low-risk HPV types can cause genital warts. They appear as soft, flesh-coloured growths that may occur singly or in clusters. Genital warts are not cancer and are usually caused by different HPV types from those responsible for cancer[20].
  • High-risk HPV types can cause cancer but they usually cause no symptoms. This is one of the most important things to understand about HPV. A woman may have a persistent high-risk HPV infection and develop precancerous changes in the cervix while staying asymptomatic (sometimes for up to 15 to 20 years)[5].
  • symptoms such as bleeding between periods, after sex or after menopause may be present in early stages, foul smelling vaginal discharge, and pelvic pain may appear in advanced cases[5]. These are signs of cervical disease and not HPV infection itself. Learn more about these symptoms on our cervical cancer symptoms page.

Vaccination changes risk by preventing the infection, which is why timing matters.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) vaccine recommendations include the following[21]:

  • Girls aged 9 to 14 years (one or two doses) before their sexual debut
  • Girls and women aged 15 to 20 years (one or two doses)
  • Women older than 21 years (two doses at six months apart)
  • Immunocompromised individuals, including those living with HIV (at least two doses and, where possible, three doses)
  • Alternative single dose (off label) - single dose schedule can be used for girls and boys aged 9-20 years

An Indian multicentre study found that a single dose of the quadrivalent HPV vaccine provided 95.4% efficacy against persistent HPV-16/18 infection after long-term follow-up, which is comparable to two- and three-dose schedules[22].

The India picture

India’s national HPV vaccination campaign uses the quadrivalent Gardasil vaccine, which protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16 and 18 and is administered as a single dose[2]. Some important points include:

  • HPV vaccination was introduced in India in 2008 but faced safety concerns, misinformation, sociocultural resistance and logistical challenges[23]. Since then, extensive safety data from millions of doses worldwide have strengthened confidence in its safety. Common side effects include injection-site pain, mild fever, headache and occasional fainting, which is why brief observation after vaccination is recommended. Serious adverse events are very rare[24].
  • India’s current national HPV vaccination programme targets 14 year old girls only and does not currently include boys[2].
  • Research has also found no evidence that HPV vaccination encourages earlier sexual activity in teenagers[25]. Therefore, parents are encouraged to discuss any concerns with their doctor before vaccination and not avoid/delay vaccinations due to such fears.
  • Under the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD), cervical cancer screening is offered to women aged 30 to 65 years at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs and primary health centres, primarily using visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA). As of 20 July 2025, over 10.18 crore women had been screened[26]. However, overall screening coverage among eligible women remains low.
  • Vaccination is only one part of the strategy because screening and timely treatment are equally essential. WHO’s cervical cancer elimination strategy sets three targets: 90% of girls fully vaccinated by age 15, 70% of women screened with a high-performance test by ages 35 and 45, and 90% of women with cervical disease receiving treatment (90% of women with per cancer are treated and 90% of women with invasive cancer are managed)[27].

Measures to protect and prevent

Here’s what you can do to protect yourself and your family against cervical cancer:

  • Get eligible girls vaccinated: The campaign offers free HPV vaccination at government health facilities to girls aged 14. Girls turning 15 within 90 days of the launch are also eligible. You can register through the U-WIN portal with the help of health worker or by visiting a vaccination centre. The programme aligns with the Government’s vision of ‘Swasth Nari Sashakt Parivar ’, placing prevention, protection and equitable access at the heart of women’s healthcare to promote healthier families[1]. HPV vaccination is available at private sectors for those outside the government programme’s age group[28]. To know more, discuss its suitability at your age with a doctor, as protection is best done before exposure to HPV. Our pages on the cervical cancer vaccine: benefits, age limit and side effects and what the HPV vaccine is and when to take it cover the practical questions parents ask most often.
  • Continue screening, whether vaccinated or not: Those vaccinated as girls still need cervical screening as an adult[29]. Cervical screening methods primarily include HPV DNA testing, with LBC (liquid based cytology) , colposcopy and VIA (visual inspection with acetic acid as triage test[30]. Our screening page explains what the process involves, while the risk and prevention hub covers the broader picture. PharmEasy's cancer screening package for women and its more extensive advanced women’s cancer screening package offer private blood-based checks and tumour markers. It is important to understand their limits, as neither package provides cervical screening. No blood test or tumour marker can confirm or rule out cancer on its own. Doctors use these markers to support diagnosis, monitor treatment response, and check whether cancer has returned in someone already under care[31].
  • Contact the doctor promptly if you notice[32]:
    • Vaginal bleeding between periods, after sex or after menopause.
  • Unusual vaginal discharge that persists, particularly if it contains blood or smells unpleasant.
    • Pelvic pain or any pain occurring during sex.
    • New pelvic pain that does not go away.
    • Painful bowel movements or pain while urinating
    • Swelling in the legs
    • Pain while urinating
    • Abdominal pain or back pain
  • Arrange an appointment if[33]:
    • Your daughter or son is in or near the eligible age group, and you want to discuss vaccination.
    • You are over 30 and request cervical screening.
    • You have received an abnormal screening result and are unsure about the next steps.
    • You have a weakened immune system and need advice about how many vaccine doses you need.

If visiting a clinic is difficult, an online doctor consultation can help you get medical advice about vaccination eligibility, screening intervals or an abnormal result.

What the evidence does not support

Several myths about HPV and its vaccine can cause unnecessary worry and confusion. Here are the actual facts behind some common misconceptions.

  • Vaccination replaces screening: A common misconception among people is that vaccination can replace screening which is absolutely wrong. The quadrivalent vaccine targets HPV types 16 and 18 which cause most but not all cervical cancers. So, women vaccinated as girls still need cervical screening as adults[29].
  • Vulval cancer is the same as HPV cancer: Other than HPV, vulval cancer may also be associated with lichen sclerosus or TP53 gene mutations. Vulval cancer associated with lichen sclerosus or TP53 mutations is not prevented by HPV vaccination[14], [34]. So, not all vulval cancers are HPV-related
  • Genital warts signal developing cancer:no, 90 percent of genital warts are caused by low-risk HPV types 6 and 11, which are different from the types that cause cancer[7].
  • HPV vaccination causes infertility: Monitoring of vaccine side effects has not revealed a link between HPV vaccination and infertility. Additionally, across many countries, safety monitoring has not identified serious safety concerns with HPV vaccines[24].
  • Two or three doses are necessarily required: Latest recommendations by WHO , suggest that one dose provides reliable protection . However, people with a weak immune system need at least two doses and if possible three doses of the HPV vaccine for better protection[35].
  • India has no national programme or that the campaign uses Cervavac: India now has a nationwide HPV vaccination programme for 14-year-old girls that offers free Gardasil-4 vaccination (not Cervavac) at government health facilities[2], [26].
  • There is a treatment for HPV itself. There is no role of antiviral treatment for for Hpv infection. Instead, treatment addresses the health problems caused by HPV.[5], [7]

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the full form of HPV?

HPV stands for human papillomavirus. It is a group of more than 200 related viruses, out of which more than 40 types may be transmitted through sexual contact.[7], [8]

What is the right age for the HPV vaccine?

India’s national programme offers HPV vaccination to girls aged 14 years[2]. WHO recommends vaccinating girls aged 9 to 14years, before sexual activity begins, as the vaccine provides the greatest protection before exposure to HPV[35].

How many doses does the HPV vaccine need?

India's vaccination programme administers a single dose. In April 2022, WHO recommended single-dose schedules for girls aged 9 to 14[21]. This is based partly on an IARC cohort study of around 17,729 adults in India followed for 10 years[22]. People with weakened immune systems may need two to three doses[21].

Is there an HPV test, and should I have one?

HPV DNA testing is available and is used in several national screening programmes[26]. If you choose private screening, ask your doctor which test will be used and how often you should repeat it.

Is there a treatment for HPV itself?

No antiviral treatment clears HPV infection. Treatment instead addresses the problems HPV causes, such as genital warts and precancerous cervical changes, which may be treated using procedures such as thermal ablation, conization[5]

Can men get the HPV vaccine in India?

Yes, boys and men can receive HPV vaccine, however, India’s national programme covers only girls aged 14[2]. Several other countries include boys in their routine vaccination programmes[36]. Boys and men in India can receive vaccination in private sectors.

If I am vaccinated, do I still need cervical screening?

Yes you still need screening. The quadrivalent vaccine does not protect against every high-risk HPV type, so vaccinated women still need cervical screening.[24], [29] Believing that vaccination replaces screening is the most common and most consequential misunderstanding about the vaccine.

Does the HPV vaccine cause infertility?

Safety monitoring since the vaccines were approved, have found no evidence that they cause infertility. Additionally, monitoring across many countries and in very large numbers of doses has not identified serious safety concerns with HPV vaccines[24].
